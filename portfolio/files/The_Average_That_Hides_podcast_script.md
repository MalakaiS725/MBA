# The Average That Hides
### A podcast episode on channel economics, attribution error, and why a healthy blended number is the most dangerous number on the report

*Runtime: roughly 18 to 20 minutes at conversational pace. Two hosts. Swap the names for whatever you want. To cut it to about 14, drop Part six and fold the recommendation into the closing.*

---

**HOST A:** I want to start with a number that should make everybody relax, and then I want to explain why it's the problem.

**HOST B:** Go.

**HOST A:** A company is spending about one point three million dollars a month to acquire customers. Across every channel they can measure, they're getting back nine dollars and sixty cents in customer lifetime value for every dollar of acquisition cost.

**HOST B:** Nine point six to one.

**HOST A:** Nine point six to one. The benchmark most people use is three to one. So they're at more than triple the bar. If you put that number in front of an investor, the meeting ends early and everybody's happy.

**HOST B:** And you're about to tell me the company is in trouble.

**HOST A:** Not in trouble. But roughly eight hundred thousand dollars a month is going somewhere it shouldn't, and the nine point six is the reason nobody noticed. That's the episode. This comes out of a digital analytics audit our group ran on Oura, the smart ring company.

**HOST B:** Let's set up what an audit like this actually is, because I think people hear "audit" and picture an accountant.

**HOST A:** Fair. Three parts. Part one, where does traffic come from and how does that compare to competitors. Part two, is the analytics setup measuring any of this correctly. Part three, what does a customer cost and what are they worth. We built a written report, an executive deck, and an interactive dashboard that holds all the data live.

**HOST B:** And one thing before we go further, because it matters.

**HOST A:** Yeah, say it.

**HOST B:** These figures are modeled. They're built from Oura's published pricing and retention and from public third-party traffic estimates. We don't have access to Oura's actual Google Analytics or their ad accounts. Nobody outside the company does.

**HOST A:** Which is disclosed on every surface. It's in the dashboard footer, it's in Appendix B of the report. We'd rather say it three times than have somebody discover it.

## Part one: the ratio and why it's three

**HOST B:** Okay. Explain the three to one, because that's the spine of the whole thing.

**HOST A:** Two numbers. CAC, customer acquisition cost, is what you pay to get one new customer. CLV, customer lifetime value, is what that customer is worth to you over some window. We used three years.

**HOST B:** And for Oura, three-year CLV is what?

**HOST A:** Three hundred twenty-two dollars and fifty-eight cents. That's built bottom up. A three hundred forty-nine dollar ring at about forty-six percent gross margin. A sixty-nine ninety-nine annual membership at ninety percent margin. Eighty-five percent retention at twelve months, which Oura has disclosed, carried forward.

**HOST B:** So the ratio is three twenty-two divided by whatever you paid.

**HOST A:** Right. And here's the part people skip. Why three? Why isn't one to one fine? If a customer is worth three hundred and you paid two hundred, you made a hundred dollars.

**HOST B:** On paper.

**HOST A:** On paper. But that three hundred twenty-two is not money you keep. It still has to cover the cost of actually serving that person for three years. Overhead. The customers who cancel in month four and never get to year two. And the fact that the revenue trickles in over thirty-six months while the ad spend left your bank account today.

**HOST B:** So three to one is the margin of safety.

**HOST A:** Three to one is the rule of thumb for a channel that genuinely pays for itself with room left over. Below three, you're buying customers who barely cover their own cost. You're busy, not profitable.

**HOST B:** And where does three twenty-two divided by three land?

**HOST A:** A hundred and seven dollars and fifty-three cents. That's the ceiling. Pay more than a hundred and seven to acquire an Oura customer and the channel doesn't clear the bar. That line is drawn right on the first chart of the dashboard, and every bar is measured against it.

---

## Part two: what the average was hiding

**HOST B:** So let's break the nine point six apart.

**HOST A:** Eight channels total. Only four of them have spend you can actually attribute. Paid search, paid social, organic search, and email. The other four, direct, referral, organic social, display, carry no attributable cost, so you literally cannot form a cost per customer for them.

**HOST B:** That's its own finding.

**HOST A:** That's its own finding, and we'll come back to it. But of the four you can measure, two clear the bar and two don't. Organic search comes in at forty-three to one. Email at twenty-one to one.

**HOST B:** Those are enormous.

**HOST A:** Seven dollars and forty-eight cents to acquire a customer through organic. Fifteen twenty-eight through email. Payback in under a month in both cases.

**HOST B:** And the two that fail.

**HOST A:** Paid search, a hundred sixty-seven twenty-seven per customer. That's one point nine three to one. Paid social, a hundred forty-five seventy-six. Two point two one to one.

**HOST B:** So both under three.

**HOST A:** Both under three. And paid search takes nine months to pay back. Nine months, on a three-year value that assumes they don't cancel.

**HOST B:** Okay but two out of four failing is just, that's a mixed bag. That happens.

**HOST A:** It would be, if the money were spread evenly. It isn't. Here's the number that reframes the whole thing. Those two failing channels take seventy-nine point seven percent of the measured budget.

**HOST B:** Eighty percent of the money is in the channels that don't clear the bar.

**HOST A:** Eighty percent of the money. And they return sixteen and a half percent of the new customers.

**HOST B:** Say the other half.

**HOST A:** The two channels that pass take twenty percent of the budget and deliver eighty-three and a half percent of the customers.

**HOST B:** That's almost a perfect inversion.

**HOST A:** It's close to a perfect inversion. And if you want it in one sentence: a customer from the failing channels costs a hundred sixty-two dollars. A customer from the passing channels costs eight dollars. Twenty times the price for the same customer.

**HOST B:** And blended, all of that averages to nine point six.

**HOST A:** All of that averages to nine point six to one, which looks fantastic. That's the lesson of the whole audit, honestly. The average isn't wrong. It's just load-bearing camouflage. A forty-three to one channel carrying a one point nine three to one channel produces a number that ends the conversation before anybody opens it up.

## Part three: buying what somebody else earns

**HOST B:** So why is paid search so expensive for them? Is the category just competitive?

**HOST A:** That's the obvious explanation and it's the wrong one, which is what makes this interesting. You can test it by looking at the nearest competitor. Whoop.

**HOST B:** Same category, same buyer.

**HOST A:** Same category, roughly the same customer. Oura runs two hundred twenty-one thousand paid search sessions a month. Whoop runs fifteen thousand eight hundred.

**HOST B:** Wait. Fifteen thousand.

**HOST A:** Fifteen thousand eight hundred forty-four. Oura is running roughly fourteen times the paid search volume of its closest competitor in the same auction.

**HOST B:** So it's not the category. Whoop just isn't playing that game.

**HOST A:** Whoop is playing a different one. Look at where their traffic comes from. Forty-eight percent of Whoop's sessions come from organic search. For Oura it's thirty-nine percent.

**HOST B:** Oura's bigger overall though, right?

**HOST A:** Oura's bigger overall. Six point five million monthly visits against Whoop's four point seven. Fifty-five percent share of voice across the three brands we tracked. On the competitive tab, Oura leads on basically every volume metric.

**HOST B:** So what's the problem?

**HOST A:** Whoop generates more absolute organic search traffic. Two point four seven million organic sessions against Oura's two point one eight. On twenty-nine percent fewer total visits.

**HOST B:** Smaller site, more organic search traffic.

**HOST A:** Smaller site, more organic search traffic, while spending almost nothing on paid search. And then there's the split that actually explains it. Of Oura's organic traffic, seventy-eight percent is branded. People typing "Oura ring."

**HOST B:** People who already know the brand.

**HOST A:** People who were already coming. For Whoop it's seventy-two percent branded, which means their non-branded organic, the stuff that reaches people who don't know you yet, is six hundred ninety-one thousand sessions. Oura's is four hundred seventy-nine thousand.

**HOST B:** So Whoop earns more new-person discovery through search, and Oura buys it.

**HOST A:** That's the thesis of the whole audit in one line. Oura is buying the traffic position Whoop earns. And that's why the paid search CAC is a hundred sixty-seven dollars. You're paying a premium to rent demand that your competitor owns outright.

## Part four: the measurement is also wrong

**HOST B:** Okay, part two of the audit. Attribution. Set it up.

**HOST A:** Attribution is how you decide which channel gets credit when somebody converts. Because almost nobody converts on one touch. They see an Instagram ad, they read a review, they search your name two weeks later, they buy.

**HOST B:** And last click gives everything to the final step.

**HOST A:** Last click gives a hundred percent of the credit to whatever they touched last. Which is almost always a branded search or a direct visit, because that's how people finish a purchase.

**HOST B:** So the last step gets credit for work the first step did.

**HOST A:** Exactly. Data-driven attribution tries to fix that by modeling the actual contribution of each touchpoint across thousands of real paths. And when you run both side by side, which is the attribution tab of the dashboard, you can see how far off the default report is.

**HOST B:** Give me the numbers.

**HOST A:** Direct is over-credited by eighteen point seven percentage points under last click. Paid search is over-credited by six point nine points. Meanwhile paid social is under-credited by nine point one, and organic search by six point four.

**HOST B:** So paid social looks worse than it is.

**HOST A:** Paid social is almost pure discovery. It's the first thing somebody sees. Last click essentially cannot see it at all, it gets two percent of credit under last click. Under data-driven it gets eleven.

**HOST B:** And paid search looks better than it is.

**HOST A:** Here's the sentence I'd put on the slide and honestly did. The channel that fails the economics is the same channel the default report flatters. The measurement error and the overspend point in the same direction.

**HOST B:** Which means if you'd only looked at the standard report, you'd have concluded paid search was your best channel.

**HOST A:** You'd have scaled it. And there's a wrinkle here that I think is the single most useful thing in the deck for anybody actually working in this.

**HOST B:** Go.

**HOST A:** Google retired first click, linear, time decay, and position-based attribution in GA4 in November 2023. They're gone. You get data-driven, and you get two flavors of last click. That's it.

**HOST B:** So the classic five-model comparison chart that's in every textbook.

**HOST A:** You cannot run it in the product anymore. We show all five in the dashboard table because the comparison is instructive, but three of those columns are modeled, and we label them as such. If you walk into a job interview and say you'll compare attribution models in GA4, you're describing a feature that hasn't existed for three years.

**HOST B:** That's a good thing to know before the interview.

**HOST A:** Also: data-driven attribution needs roughly four hundred conversions per key event in a twenty-eight day window. Under that threshold Google quietly falls back to last click and doesn't make a fuss about telling you. So a smaller advertiser who thinks they're running DDA may not be.

## Part five: the biggest number isn't in media at all

**HOST B:** Part three of the audit was the funnel. And you told me before we recorded that this is where the actual money is.

**HOST A:** This is the part that surprised me. Six and a half million sessions a month. Forty-two percent of them reach a product page. Of those, seven and a half percent add to cart.

**HOST B:** Seven and a half.

**HOST A:** Seven and a half. Then fifty-eight percent start checkout, and fifty-six percent of those finish. End to end, sixty-six thousand seven hundred and seven purchases. Just over one percent.

**HOST B:** Where's the biggest hole?

**HOST A:** Depends what you mean by biggest, and the distinction matters. The largest absolute loss is session to product page. Three point eight million people arrive and never look at the product. The steepest rate drop is product page to cart, that seven and a half percent, which makes sense for a three hundred forty-nine dollar purchase with a sizing kit step before you can commit.

**HOST B:** But neither of those is the one you're excited about.

**HOST A:** Neither of those is the one. Split the traffic by device. Mobile is seventy-eight and a half percent of the traffic and converts at zero point eight one percent. Desktop is twenty-one and a half percent and converts at one point eight two.

**HOST B:** Mobile converts at less than half the desktop rate.

**HOST A:** Forty-four percent of it. And mobile is where almost all the traffic is. So run the arithmetic. The gap is one point zero one percentage points. Close a conservative ten percent of that gap.

**HOST B:** Ten percent. Not closing it, just a tenth of it.

**HOST A:** A tenth of it. That's about five thousand one hundred seventy additional purchases a month. At the one-year CLV of two hundred twenty-three fifty-three, that's one point one six million dollars of customer value. Every month.

**HOST B:** Compared to the paid search problem, which is what, eight hundred thousand a month of spend.

**HOST A:** And the recommended reallocation only moves two hundred eighty-five thousand of it. So the mobile conversion fix is a larger prize than the entire media reallocation, and it requires no additional media spend at all. It's a product and checkout problem, not a marketing problem.

**HOST B:** Which nobody would have found by looking at channel performance.

**HOST A:** Which is why the funnel tab exists.

## Part six: what we actually recommended

**HOST B:** Last slide of the deck. What's the ask.

**HOST A:** Cut two hundred eighty-five thousand a month from non-brand paid search. Not all of it. Hold the brand-defense spend, because if you stop bidding on your own name while your organic brand position is unresolved, a competitor buys that real estate.

**HOST B:** Where does the money go?

**HOST A:** A hundred and fifty thousand into organic and content, which is the forty-three to one channel and also the one that's declining. Sixty thousand into lifecycle and CRM, the email side, twenty-one to one, and membership retention is literally the entire second and third year of that CLV figure.

**HOST B:** And paid social? It failed too.

**HOST A:** Hold it, and remeasure it on data-driven attribution first. Do not cut a channel you've just demonstrated you're mismeasuring by nine points. That's the discipline. You cut what you've measured correctly, not what looks worst on a broken report.

**HOST B:** So net?

**HOST A:** Net seventy-five thousand a month out. Spend drops five point eight percent. Blended CAC goes from thirty-three fifty-nine to an estimated twenty-seven forty, an eighteen percent improvement. Payback shortens from one point eight months to one point five.

**HOST B:** And the saving isn't the point.

**HOST A:** The saving isn't the point. The point is that the channel generating most of the acquisition was getting a fifth of the budget, and now it gets more.

## Closing

**HOST B:** If somebody takes one thing out of this episode.

**HOST A:** Be suspicious of a healthy average. Nine point six to one was true, defensible, and completely concealed the fact that eighty percent of the budget was in channels returning sixteen percent of the customers. Nobody falsified anything. The aggregation did the hiding.

**HOST B:** And the second thing?

**HOST A:** Check what your tools actually do before you build a method on them. Half the attribution analysis people describe confidently cannot be run in GA4 anymore, and has not been runnable since 2023.

**HOST B:** And the third.

**HOST A:** The biggest number in a marketing audit was not a marketing number. It was a mobile checkout experience converting at less than half the rate of desktop, sitting there quietly, worth more than the entire media reallocation we spent three weeks arguing about.

**HOST B:** Where can people see the dashboard?

**HOST A:** It's live, it's public, five tabs, everything we just talked through is in there with the data behind every chart. And the provenance note is on it, because everything we just said is modeled, not measured. Thanks for listening.
